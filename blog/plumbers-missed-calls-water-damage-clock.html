They call a plumber, and if no one picks up, they dial the next one. The job goes to whoever answers.

Why is the phone harder to answer in plumbing than in almost any trade?

Because the person who does the work is the same person who would answer the phone, and plumbing work makes you unreachable. You are on your back under a vanity, arms deep in a drain, in a crawlspace, or shutting off a main. Your hands are wet and full. Industry estimates put the share of calls home-service contractors miss during work hours anywhere from a quarter to well over half, and plumbers sit at the rough end of that range because the job physically pulls you away from the phone.

Then there is the after-hours gap. Plumbing emergencies do not keep business hours. Pipes freeze and burst overnight. Water heaters fail on weekends. Drains back up at dinner. A large share of plumbing calls, well over half in many shops, come outside a nine-to-five window. If your phone rolls to voicemail every evening and all weekend, you are closed during exactly the hours your highest-urgency, highest-value calls come in.

What does the water-damage clock have to do with it?

Everything, and this is what makes plumbing different from every other trade. A homeowner with a burst pipe is not just choosing a plumber. They are watching water spread across a floor, soak into drywall, and run toward the finished rooms below. Every minute you do not answer, their damage grows and their panic climbs. They will not sit through four rings and a voicemail greeting while the water rises. They will hang up before the beep and call the next number.

That urgency cuts both ways. It is why a missed plumbing call is so easy to lose, and it is why an answered one converts so well. The customer is not comparison shopping on price. They want the first competent person who picks up and says “we can get someone to you.” Industry data shows roughly 78% of customers hire whoever responds first. In plumbing, with the clock running on real property damage, that instinct is even stronger.

What does a missed call actually cost a plumber?

A single missed call is rarely a small job. Plumbing tickets run higher than a lot of trades because the work is urgent and often involves real damage: emergency call-out fees, water heater replacements, repipes, sewer line work. Industry figures put the average value of a missed home-service call in the hundreds of dollars, and for plumbing the emergency jobs sit well above that. Estimates land many contractors in the tens of thousands of dollars a year in lost revenue from calls that never got answered.

Do the math on your own shop. If you miss five callable jobs a week and each is worth even $400 to $500 on the first visit, that is well over $100,000 a year walking out the door before you count the water heaters, repipes, and service agreements those customers might have signed.

And the worst part is you never see it. A missed call leaves no record and no report. The lead just quietly becomes the customer of whoever picked up.

Why won't callers just leave a voicemail?

Because they cannot afford to. When someone is watching water pour out of a wall, a voicemail box is useless to them. They need a human answer now, and they will keep dialing until they get one. Most callers will not leave a message at all. They hang up and call the next plumber, and by the time you check your voicemail that evening, the job is done, the water is cleaned up, and the customer belongs to a competitor.

This is why “we return every call the next morning” is not a strategy in plumbing. The next morning is far too late for an emergency, and it is usually too late for the routine jobs too. The plumber who answers wins the work. Full stop.

How does an AI receptionist handle emergency and after-hours calls?

An AI receptionist answers your phone the moment your team cannot. It picks up on the first ring, greets the caller in your business name, and handles the conversation the way a trained dispatcher would. For a plumbing shop, a good one should:

  • Answer every call 24/7, including nights, weekends, and the middle of a job when your hands are full.
  • Triage the emergency first: is water actively flowing, where is the shutoff, is it a burst pipe, a sewage backup, a failed water heater, residential or commercial, what is the address.
  • Walk a panicked caller through shutting off the water while help is on the way, so the damage stops climbing before your tech even arrives.
  • Book the job straight into your scheduling tool, whether that is Google Calendar, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan, Jobber, or Calendly.
  • Flag true emergencies and route them to you or your on-call tech right away, instead of letting a flooding basement sit in a voicemail box.
  • Capture the caller's name, number, and job details so you have a clean lead even when the next open slot is tomorrow.

The point is not to sound like a robot reading a script. The point is to make sure the customer standing in water gets a real answer and a real plan, so they never feel the need to call the next plumber.

More than 43 million people in the US speak Spanish at home, and in many service areas a real share of your inbound calls are from Spanish-speaking homeowners and property managers.
